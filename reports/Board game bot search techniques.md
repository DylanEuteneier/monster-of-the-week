# Spend the search where the decisions are

The strongest bots in published card and board game work do not run cleverer search. They put a modest search budget on a well-chosen short list of moves and score the ends of play-outs with a good evaluation, and that is the shape the Monster of the Week bots already have. The next gains are, in order: find out how far more search keeps paying (the 8 × 6 jump says the budget had been binding); make sure the short list contains the move the play-outs would pick, first by measuring how often it misses, then by learning a ranker from the play-outs' own choices; make the play-out bots stop making obvious mistakes with big cards (`deepRoll`, already built); and only then refit the end-of-round evaluation on a much larger log from the current bots. Commercial practice points the same way. Race for the Galaxy shipped a learned evaluation with 1–2 ply lookahead. Dominion uses a learned prior to decide which branches get search. Board Game Arena has no AI framework at all, and its bots are scripted solo-mode rules. Information-set MCTS, multiplayer search trees, paranoid opponent models and neural self-play all have evidence against them at our budget. One finding matters for data quality more than for strength: the play-outs currently run on the true game state, so the bots see rivals' hands and profiles. A determinized version (rivals' unseen cards redealt in each play-out, consistent with what the bot has seen) is the honest baseline for card probes and for any human-facing claim, and it should be gated as a non-regression test, not a strength test. Findings tied to a source are cited inline. Fit to MotW, costs and expected payoffs are inference unless they cite the project's own logs.

## The bots already sit on the design the evidence favours

Our bot is flat Monte Carlo at the root: a hand-built evaluation, a candidate listing, a one-step score to a short list of 8, then 6 play-outs each to the end of the round by fast bots. The search literature broadly endorses this shape for low budgets. In microRTS, "when the computational budget is very low, MC [flat] seems to outperform MCTS, while as the computation budget increases this is reversed", and ε-greedy flat Monte Carlo was the best choice at about 500 play-outs ([Ontañón 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.04805)). In a study of four partially observable games (10,000–25,000 play-outs, at least 1,000 games each), "only in Go Fish did performing search improve performance over flat Monte-Carlo sampling" ([Pepels, Cazenave & Winands](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/po-hybrid.pdf)). Cheating or determinized flat Monte Carlo also holds up commercially. A commercial Dou Di Zhu AI built on flat Monte Carlo plus heuristics drew level with both information-set MCTS and determinized UCT once it was given **100× its normal simulations** ([Cowling, Powley & Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)).

The upgrades the field usually reaches for next carry costs we cannot pay. Information-set MCTS fixes strategy fusion (different sampled worlds picking different moves from states the bot cannot tell apart), but it is **two to four times slower per iteration**. At equal time it was slightly worse than determinized UCT at 1 s in Lord of the Rings: The Confrontation and only pulled ahead from 3 s up. In Dou Di Zhu the two were on a par: 43.6% for determinized UCT against 42.3% for ISMCTS ([Cowling et al. 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)). For multiplayer trees, MCTS with max^n backups (each seat maximises its own score) "clearly performs best", ahead of paranoid and best-reply trees, whose advantages come from alpha-beta pruning ([Nijssen & Winands](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/Multi_Overview.pdf)). Our play-outs already have each seat play its own profile, which is the flat analogue of max^n. At low budgets, tabletop-game MCTS often does as well or better when it ignores opponents in its own tree (MultiTree or Self-only MCTS), with the advantage fading as the budget grows ([Goodman, Perez-Liebana & Lucas 2022](https://diego-perez.net/assets/pdf/papers/MultitreeMCTSTabletop-CoG22.pdf)). Rolling-horizon evolution "still falls behind" one-step lookahead in hidden-information tabletop games ([Gaina et al. 2020, TAG](https://diego-perez.net/assets/pdf/papers/TAG_Tabletop_Games_Framework.pdf)). None of these is a step up for a 3–5 player game at tens of play-outs per decision.

The project's own logs back the literature's warnings on two points. A play-out into the next round (`deep`) went 0 for 20 as a single trophy challenger, and backers fell 15 to 17 points a seat ([experiment catalogue](../experiments/CATALOGUE.md)). That is consistent with TAG's finding that games with direct counter-play favour short rollouts ([Goodman et al., FDG 2023](https://diego-perez.net/assets/pdf/papers/FollowingLeader-FDG23.pdf)). The `field` switch (the win chance against every rival, multiplied) measured worse: trophy 21% against 29% a seat, backers 8% against 29%. The literature's equivalent is the "Leader" reward (own score minus the best opponent's score), which was the best reward in tactically interactive games ([Goodman et al., FDG 2023](https://diego-perez.net/assets/pdf/papers/FollowingLeader-FDG23.pdf)). The standard margin against the best rival is that reward, so the evidence agrees with keeping it.

## Platforms ship scripts or learned evaluators, not deep search

Board Game Arena is not a source of technique. Its developer wiki states: "There is no framework support (now) for AI/Bots, so it is all custom stuff." The bot modes in games such as The Crew, Tapestry and Glow are "Automa" solo rules. The only production approach the wiki calls feasible is fake players simulated inside the PHP game-state machine, with no persistent AI process ([BGA wiki](https://en.doc.boardgamearena.com/Bots_and_Artificial_Intelligence)). The general game systems ship menus. Ludii offers flat Monte Carlo, UCT and variants, and alpha-beta that "uses a paranoid search" for more than two players. A meta-agent picks the best of these per game from offline results ([LudiiAI](https://github.com/Ludeme/LudiiAI)). OpenSpiel lists IS-MCTS, max^n and perfect-information Monte Carlo, but marks all three "lightly tested" ([OpenSpiel algorithms](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/deepmind/open_spiel/master/docs/algorithms.md)). Yucata, boardgamecore and Tabletop Simulator turned up no bot material. The search did not find whether they have bots at all.

The two documented strong commercial AIs both come from Temple Gates. Keldon Jones' Race for the Galaxy AI is a temporal-difference neural evaluation trained by self-play over about 30,000 games. It ships as **24 networks** (an evaluator and an opponent-role predictor for each of 12 expansion and player-count settings) and chooses moves with "a full 2 ply lookahead". Easier difficulty levels add noise to the evaluation's scores, and the medium bot is set to win about 25% of games against hard ([Temple Gates](http://www.templegatesgames.com/race-for-the-galaxy-ai/)). Its Dominion AI is AlphaZero-style. The network outputs a value for each player plus a weight for each choice. A Workshop with six eligible cards gets six branch weights, and those weights decide where the tree search looks. The developers' named pitfalls were that one-turn lookahead is too short for Dominion, and that "random MCTS hides strong low-weighted branches" while hand-authored pruning is capped by the developer's own skill ([GameTek](https://gametek.substack.com/p/big-brain-small-phone-ai-for-dominion)). For Root, Twilight Struggle, Terraforming Mars and Through the Ages there are reviews but no method write-ups. Players call Terraforming Mars' hard AI "really easy to beat" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/800270/discussions/0/2243301553207313114)). The commercial lesson for MotW is narrow and consistent. A strong bot combines a good evaluation with a prior that puts the search effort on the right branches. Weaker bots are cheap to make afterwards by adding noise.

## The budget, the short list and the play-out bots are where strength is now

### More search paid once; map the curve before buying more

The 8 × 6 result (backers 40% a seat against 5%, trophy 38% against 16%, 40 games each) is the biggest change since play-outs were introduced. It reverses the 2026-10-10 reading, when 8 play-outs, 10 kept moves and successive halving all came out inconclusive ([design doc](../docs/motw-design.md)). The likely reason is that the game changed underneath the bots. The unsuited cards have many uses and long target lists, and before `even` the listing ran out on 86–94% of turns for three of them ([design doc](../docs/motw-design.md)). The published curves say gains from budget are large and then flatten. In Settlers of Catan, one MCTS player against three JSettlers bots won **27% at 1,000 simulations and 49% at 10,000** ([Szita, Chaslot & Spronck 2010](https://www.spronck.net/pubs/ACG12Szita.pdf)). In Magic: The Gathering, a larger budget was "best used in running additional determinizations rather than searching each determinization more deeply". Without restructuring the moves, going from 10k to 100k simulations gave "no better and indeed slightly worse" play ([Cowling, Ward & Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)).

The first job is therefore a budget map, not a new algorithm. Run four arms that trade breadth for depth around the new standard: 12 × 6, 8 × 12, 16 × 6 and 12 × 8. Each is a `keep`/`playouts` profile string and needs no code. That separates two hypotheses. If wider short lists win, the short list is missing good moves (recall). If more play-outs per move win, the play-out averages are too noisy to rank close moves. Two cautions come from the logs. The 5% backer baseline is low against an even share of 20%, and the `rank` switch's first gain did not repeat on a fresh seed, so the 8 × 6 effect is probably smaller than measured and the map doubles as its replication. And when budget arms differ in speed, record time per game alongside wins, so the designer can decide where the curve flattens.

Successive halving should be retested at the new budget rather than written off. It is parameter-free and, in the partially observable games tested, it "either outperforms MCTS and UCB significantly, or does not negatively impact performance". It added up to 7–9 points in the phantom games ([Pepels, Cazenave & Winands](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/po-hybrid.pdf)). But that evidence is at 10,000 play-outs, and our 2026-10-10 trials ran at 24, where 2 play-outs an arm in the first round is mostly noise. At 48 play-outs, halving over 16 moves (3 each, then 6, then 12) also buys breadth at no extra cost. The `halve` switch already exists.

### The short list is the decision; measure its recall, then learn it

The strongest single result in card-game search is about which moves the search considers, not how it searches. In Hanabi, limiting the tree's moves to a handful of rule-generated options raised re-determinizing ISMCTS **from about 4.2 to 17.4 points out of 25** at 100 ms ([Goodman 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.06075)). In Magic, splitting each card choice into a chain of yes/no decisions lifted the win rate against the expert-rules player **from 37.3% to 52.0%** while halving the time per move ([Cowling, Ward & Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)). TAG's Terraforming Mars splits "play a card" into separate payment and effect decisions "to avoid extremely large combinatorial action spaces". There, MCTS beat one-step lookahead 75% head to head ([Gaina, Goodman & Perez-Liebana 2021](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/18902/18902-52-22668-1-2-20211004.pdf)). With cards offering 1–400 targets, our candidate listing plus one-step score is exactly this kind of hard pruning. A move cut there never reaches a play-out, however large the budget.

The cheapest step is a recall measurement, with no change to play. On a sample of decisions, play out a wide list (say 24 moves × 6) and record where the play-outs' winner sat in the one-step ranking and in the listing. If the winner is regularly at positions 9–24, the short list is the bottleneck. If it is almost always in the top 8, the budget belongs in more play-outs. The same log answers whether `spread` and `rank` failed because their candidates were bad or because their results were noisy. Both were measured at 24 or fewer play-outs per decision, with ±6–8-point errors per seat ([design doc](../docs/motw-design.md)).

If recall is the problem, the evidence-backed fix is the expert-iteration idea, scaled down. In Hex, a policy prior learned by imitating MCTS's own choices **won 97% against plain MCTS at about twice the cost per iteration, where simply doubling the MCTS iterations won 56%**. Training on the search's full visit distribution beat training on the chosen move alone by 50 ± 13 Elo ([Anthony, Tian & Barber 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.08439)). For MotW, log each candidate's features at every searched decision: card, use, target kind, the one-step score's components, and the change in each seat's win chance. Log each short-listed move's play-out mean alongside them. Then fit a small logistic or pairwise ranker that predicts the play-out means and use it to fill the short list. It costs a feature vector and a dot product per candidate, negligible next to one play-out. It differs from the failed `rank` switch, which ranked by the evaluation with fights not projected, a cheaper version of the same judgement. A learned ranker is trained on what the expensive stage concluded. One structural variant also fits Magic's evidence: decide the card first, then the target. Give each card its best two or three targets in the first play-out round, then spend the rest of the budget on targets within the leading cards. This differs from `spread`, which sent only one move per card and then spent evenly.

### Play-out bots should avoid blunders, not hold opinions

Better play-out bots help only in one specific way. Magic's reduced-rules play-outs beat its expert-rules play-outs by **about 10 points** ([Cowling, Ward & Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)). Hand-weighted play-outs made a Catan agent "significantly" weaker ([Szita et al. 2010](https://www.spronck.net/pubs/ACG12Szita.pdf)). Yet in Lost Cities, play-outs that avoid obvious blunders improved play "by up to 40%" over random ones ([Pepels et al.](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/po-hybrid.pdf)), and in Breakthrough a policy that takes one-move wins and blocks one-move losses beat uniform play-outs 94.3% ([Lanctot et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.0486)). Making play-outs smarter also costs time: small searches inside play-outs improved their quality but slowed them enough to cancel the gain at short thinking times ([Nijssen & Winands](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/playout-search-for-monte-carlo-tree-search-in-multi-player-games)).

`deepRoll` is on the right side of that line. The imagined opponents sample 8 targets a card, so the big unsuited cards get misplayed in imagined futures, which is a blunder rather than a style. Giving play-out seats the same listing removes it. Its cost is slower play-outs, and an even comparison is a trade at equal time as well as at equal play-out count. Run both: `deepRoll=1` at 8 × 6, and the standard at whatever 8 × N matches its time. If `deepRoll` wins only at equal play-out count, it is a budget decision for the designer. Cheaper middle grounds exist if it is too slow: list targets only for the cards in the play-out seat's hand that have more than 8, or cap the listing at 16. MAST-style adaptive play-outs are cheap and gave about 70% against random play-outs in general game playing ([Sironi, Cazenave & Winands](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/EnhancingPPAforGGP.pdf)), but at tens of play-outs per decision they would learn from very few samples. Rank them low.

## The evaluation is the ceiling once search and listing are fixed

Every play-out ends in the hand-built evaluation, so its errors cap what more play-outs can buy. The logs already show it is weak in rounds 2–5. Cross-validated log-loss was 1.624–1.725 for the bots' own forecasts against 1.609 for an even split, and 1.434–1.555 for a logistic fit on 88 games. The rounds 2–3 forecasts even had Platt slopes below zero ([design doc](../docs/motw-design.md)). Yet the fitted evaluation, used in place of the goal value or blended in, came out inconclusive on 20–40 games. The literature explains both halves. Texel-style fitting of evaluation weights to game outcomes gained about **+100 Elo** in a chess engine, but on **64,000 games and 8.8 million positions**. Its biggest single gains came from regenerating the data from the current engine (+12.8) and fixing which positions were included (+39.4) ([Texel's Tuning Method](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Texel%27s_Tuning_Method)). Eighty-eight games from bots that have since changed (even listing, new cards, deeper search) is far below that, and the data has gone stale.

The higher-quality-data priority points straight at a refit. Log features from several hundred games of the current standard bots and refit per round with L2 regularisation and folds by deal. Retest `blend` as a challenger only once the cross-validated gain is clearly larger than the 88-game fit's. A Hearthstone value network used this way, to cut play-outs early and score their ends, raised the first player's win rate from **73.5% to 82.0%** ([Świechowski, Tajmajer & Janusz 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1808.04794)). In the same competition, though, plain logistic regression "did well" and the best entry was under 2 points above the baseline ([Janusz et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1708.00730)). Good features matter more than the model. A cheaper complementary trick is to keep the one-step score in the decision instead of discarding it after short-listing: mix it with the play-out mean. In Breakthrough this kind of "implicit minimax backup" (heuristic values mixed in at α ≈ 0.4) won 82.3% against MCTS with an improved play-out policy ([Lanctot et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.0486)). It is one line in `simPick` and costs nothing per decision. Neural evaluators trained by self-play remain out of reach. Race for the Galaxy needed about 30,000 self-play games for one setting ([Temple Gates](http://www.templegatesgames.com/race-for-the-galaxy-ai/)), which at a few dozen MotW games an hour per machine is weeks of compute for each rules change.

## Peeking flatters the bots and skews the data

`playOutRound` plays from the true game state. The imagined rivals play the cards actually in their hands, and `table` hands each play-out seat its rival's real profile (the code calls this "a little cheating, fine for now"). Within a round, the only randomness in a play-out is the fast bots' own noise. This is "cheating" Monte Carlo, which is stronger than any honest version. Cheating UCT scored 56.5% in Dou Di Zhu against 43.6% for determinized UCT ([Cowling et al. 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)). For a designer whose priority is data quality, that strength is the problem. Card probes and win shares from bots that know every hand overstate cards that exploit exact knowledge (responses, bluff tokens, Steal Their Playbook's copy) and understate the value of hiding information. Results will not carry over to tables of humans.

The fix comes in steps. First, redeal each rival's unseen cards in every play-out from the cards the bot cannot account for, using the hard constraints the draft provides: cards it passed, cards it saw in a pack that did not come back. This is plain determinization (perfect-information Monte Carlo). It is the method behind strong trick-taking programs, and it works best when hidden information is revealed quickly and sibling outcomes are correlated ([Long et al., AAAI 2010](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/7562)). That fits a game whose hands empty within a round. The redeal also gives the play-outs a second source of variance that should make the 6 play-outs per move more informative. Second, if the honest bot loses much strength, weight the redeals by how likely each rival's observed plays would be under the bot's own one-step scorer (a softmax over its scores). This is the Skat recipe. Policy-based inference beat no inference by **+3.5 to +10.4 tournament points per game** across game types, at about 5× the runtime of the previous method ([Rebstock et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.10911)). Third, replace the peeked profiles with a belief over each rival's goal (island, or backing faction X) updated from their plays. The evidence for opponent modelling's isolated value is thin. MCTS "generally performs well even with an inaccurate model" ([Goodman & Lucas 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08659v1)), and "the agent's own actions often have a significantly greater impact on the final score than appropriate opponent prediction" ([MCTS review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.04931)). So this step is for honesty, not strength. Every step should be gated as non-regression against the peeking bot, and bot-only win shares will probably fall a little. Adopting it is the designer's call.

Measurement shapes the order. At 40 games, one challenger seat's win share carries an error of roughly ±6–8 points. The 8 × 6 effect was large enough to see. The next steps will mostly be smaller, and `rank` already showed a first result that did not repeat. So each technique should be screened on one seed and confirmed on a fresh one before adoption. Ranks 2 and 5 produce offline numbers (recall rates, cross-validated log-loss) that need no win-share test at all. That makes them the cheapest way to decide where the expensive challenger runs go.

## Conclusion

The 8 × 6 result changes the picture from the 2026-10-10 overnight runs, which concluded that the search no longer limited the bots. The game has since grown cards whose many uses and long target lists make move selection the hard part. So for now the bottleneck is how well the bot chooses which moves to examine and how well its imagined opponents play those cards, not search depth or algorithm. That is the same lesson Hanabi, Magic and Dominion's developers drew. The work should go into the listing, the short list and the play-out bots before anything is spent on search machinery.

The second takeaway concerns what the bots are for. They exist to produce design data, and right now they know every hand. More strength from a peeking bot can make the probes less faithful, not more. A determinized bot that is slightly weaker but plays from what a player could know is the better instrument. The designer should decide which kind of truth the bots serve before their next large tuning pass.
