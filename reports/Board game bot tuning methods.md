# Pair the seeds before you tune the bots

The best path to smarter, more reliably tuned Monster of the Week bots is to fix how results are measured before changing how the bots play. A 10-game challenger run cannot detect the size of effect that most weight tweaks produce. The backers' swing between 18% and 8% a seat is about 1.4 standard errors, which is what noise alone produces. The same game budget goes several times further when results are paired by seed, the bots' own win-chance forecast is used as a luck correction, and a stopping rule decides when a challenger has won or lost. After that, the published game-AI work points to four bot-side changes, in this order: recalibrate the late-round win-chance forecast against logged outcomes; spend the play-out budget adaptively (successive halving over the top-k moves); tune 5 to 8 weights at a time with NTBEA, the noisy bandit optimiser built for game-AI budgets like ours, instead of one hand change at a time; and judge profile balance against a pool of past versions, not the current table. Deeper machinery (full information-set MCTS, neural evaluators, SPSA or CLOP tuning, smarter play-out bots) has evidence against it at our speed of about 37 games an hour. The ranked list at the end gives payoff, cost and sources for each idea. Throughout, findings tied to a source are cited inline; statements marked *inference* are reasoning from those sources or my own arithmetic, not published results.

## A 10-point seat gap is still inside the noise

At a 20% baseline, a seat's win share is a binomial proportion with a large standard error: about **6.3 points at 40 seat-games and 5.8 at 48** (*inference*, standard binomial formula). The observed backers result of 18% over 40 games against 8% over 48 games is a 10-point gap with a standard error of the difference of about 7.2 points. That is about 1.4 SE, well within the range of chance. Detecting a +5-point gain against a known 20% baseline needs about **527 challenger seat-games** (α = 0.05, power 0.8), and comparing two estimated arms needs about 1,090 per arm (*inference*, normal approximation). At 37 games an hour, one challenger seat per game is about 14 hours for the one-sample case. A 10-point effect needs a quarter of that. So the current practice of 10-game runs can only show effects of roughly 10 to 15 points or more. Most of the "balance swings between seeds" are measurement noise, not changes in the bots.

The external record agrees on how stubborn this noise is. Mark Fisher's Provincial Dominion bot, at 10,000 games per matchup, still showed a strategy scoring **−2.3% against itself**, and Fisher greys out non-significant cells in his matchup tables ([Fisher](https://graphics.stanford.edu/~mdfisher/DominionAI.html)). Chess engine tuning shows the scale of budget that tuning on win/loss alone requires: Kiiski's original Stockfish tuning used **30,000 to 100,000 games per session** ([CPW: Stockfish's Tuning Method](https://chessprogramming.org/Stockfish%27s_Tuning_Method)), and a 2024 fishtest time-management tune used 35,000 games and gained about +2 to +3 Elo ([open-chess.org](https://open-chess.org/viewtopic.php?p=34292)). We will never have that budget, so the gains have to come from extracting more information per game.

Three practical consequences follow before any bot code changes. First, report every win share with a Wilson interval. Eight wins in 40 seat-games is 20%, with a 95% interval of roughly 10% to 35% (*inference*). Second, when a challenger fills several seats in one game, pool its result per game, because only one seat can win and the seats are negatively correlated (*inference*). Third, treat 10-game runs as screens that discard clear losers, not as evidence for adopting a change. That keeps the designer's quick-iteration habit and stops small samples from settling questions they cannot answer.

## Duplicate seeds and a luck correction multiply every game

The standard first remedy, in both computer poker and chess testing, is to replay the same chance events with the seats swapped and to analyse the pair, not the game. Duplicate poker is "widely used in the Annual Computer Poker Competitions" to "mitigate the effects of luck" ([OpenHoldem, arXiv 2012.06168](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.06168)). The University of Alberta deals the same cards with the seats reversed and resets the bots between matches so they cannot remember the deals ([AAMAS13 baseline paper](https://www.cs.ualberta.ca/~games/poker/publications/AAMAS13-baseline.pdf)). In chess, treating paired games as independent overestimates the variance. The pentanomial (pair-level) model makes an SPRT test about **20% shorter** (1,809 against 2,191 expected games in one setting) while holding its nominal error rate, where the trinomial version drifts to 0.968 ([vdbergh/pentanomial](https://github.com/vdbergh/pentanomial); [CPW Match Statistics](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Match_Statistics)).

For MotW the analogue is a **5-seat duplicate block** (*inference*). Play one seed five times with the challenger rotated through every seat and standard bots in the other four. Score the block as one observation, and compare it with the standard bot's results on the same seed and seats, which is duplicate poker's "scored relative to others who held the same cards" rule ([MatchPoker](https://matchpoker.com/?p=1486)). A block costs about 65 core-minutes, so 8 cores run about 7 blocks an hour. Our `scripts/tournament.js` already rotates profiles through seats (seat *i* plays profile (*i* + *g*) mod *n*) and derives each game's seed from the run seed, so runs of two variants with the same `seed=` argument already share decks and deals game by game. The gain is realised only if the analysis takes the difference per game or per block and uses the standard deviation of those differences. Comparing two pooled win rates throws the pairing away. One engineering caveat: the tournament passes one RNG to every bot decision. Once a variant chooses differently, later random draws shift and the paired games diverge. Common random numbers work best when the deck, the deals and the search randomness each draw from their own stream (*inference*; standard practice, but I found no game-specific source). No published figure says how much variance duplicate play removes in a 5-player board game, so the first step is to measure it: run about 10 blocks and compare the block variance with the independent-games variance p(1−p)/5.

The second remedy uses the evaluator the bots already have. **AIVAT** adds, at every chance event, the difference between the expected value of the evaluator over all possible outcomes and the value of the outcome that actually happened. Each correction term has expectation zero, so the estimator is **unbiased for any value function, however badly calibrated**. A poor evaluator only costs efficiency ([AIVAT, arXiv 1612.06915](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1612.06915)). In heads-up no-limit hold'em it cut the standard deviation from 25.96 to 8.10, about **68%, or more than 10× fewer games**. The chance-node-only version (MIVAT) gave 18% to 34% ([AIVAT](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1612.06915)). A simpler cousin is CUPED: regress the outcome on a pre-treatment covariate and subtract, which cuts variance by a factor of (1 − ρ²) ([r-statistics.co CUPED](https://r-statistics.co/Variance-Reduction-with-CUPED.html); [Statsig](https://docs.statsig.com/statsig-warehouse-native/features/statistics/methodologies/cuped)). For MotW (*inference*), the cheap version is CUPED on the standard evaluator's win-chance forecast for each seat after the deck and first deal, fitting θ on both arms pooled. The bigger version is AIVAT-lite at each deal and random draw: resample a few outcomes of the chance event and evaluate them without playing them out, at a cost of evaluator calls, not play-outs. The important property for us is that **late-round over-confidence does not bias these corrections**, so the corrected comparisons stay trustworthy while the forecast is being fixed. No published application to multiplayer board games exists, so the size of the gain must also be measured here. A weakly predictive opening position (ρ = 0.3) gives only a 9% cut.

With paired units in hand, a **sequential test** replaces the fixed 10-game run. The chess SPRT updates a log-likelihood ratio after every game pair and stops at bounds of ln(β/(1−α)) and ln((1−β)/α) ([CPW Match Statistics](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Match_Statistics); [CPW SPRT](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Sequential_Probability_Ratio_Test)). Adapted to blocks (*inference*): H0 is a mean block score of 0.20, H1 is 0.25, α = β = 0.1 gives bounds of ±2.20, and the test is checked after each batch of 8 parallel blocks. Without variance reduction, the expected length is about 56 blocks (about 280 games, 7.5 hours), and every variance cut shortens it proportionally. Fishtest's "non-regression" mode (bounds such as [−1.75, 0.25] Elo) suits changes meant to simplify or speed up a bot without making it weaker ([Fishtest wiki](https://github.com/official-stockfish/fishtest/wiki/Creating-my-first-test)). That matches the standing rule that slower search must earn its cost. To choose among several variants, F-Race and Hoeffding races drop configurations once paired tests show them behind, so the games go to the close contenders ([F-race overview](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/f-race-and-iterated-f-race-an-overview/); [arXiv 1206.2248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.2248)). irace waits 5 instances before its first elimination ([irace docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/irace/html/irace.cmdline.html)). A 2022 study found no universal winner between Friedman and t-test races when samples are few ([arXiv 2204.09353](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.09353)).

## Recalibrating the forecast repairs both the bots and the card probes

The bots' late-round over-confidence shows up twice: in play, where the search trusts its own forecasts, and in the card probes, whose response is a change in that forecast. The fix is well established. Measure calibration with the Brier score and its reliability/resolution decomposition, then fit Platt scaling or temperature scaling on held-out data ([MetricGate Brier decomposition](https://metricgate.com/docs/brier-score-decomposition/); [MetricGate calibration](https://metricgate.com/blogs/brier-score-vs-log-loss-vs-calibration/)). Guo et al. found **temperature scaling, a single parameter, often the most effective fix** for over-confident models ([Guo et al., ICML 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html)). Isotonic regression is more flexible but needs more data ([MetricGate isotonic](https://metricgate.com/docs/isotonic-regression-calibration/)).

The tournament already logs a forecast for every seat at the start of each round's play, along with the eventual winners, so the data exists (*inference* from the code). The recipe (*inference*): per round, fit logit(p_cal) = a_r + b_r·logit(q). Over-confidence shows up as b_r < 1. That is two parameters per round, which tens of games can fit. Renormalise the five seats to sum to 1, split train and test by game, and check with a reliability table of five quantile bins per round. Platt is safer than isotonic at our sample sizes. One caveat: shallow, low-noise play-outs can themselves cause over-confidence, because they undersample opponents' good replies. If calibration drifts back after a fix, look at play-out diversity as well as the mapping.

For the **card probes**, the restricted-play framing of Jaffe et al. confirms the design: measure an element's importance by comparing an agent allowed it with one barred from it ([Jaffe et al., AIIDE](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12513)). Our "play the card vs skip the turn" probe on shared seeds is a better estimand than the observational "win rate when drawn" metrics. The 17Lands GIH and IWD statistics are known to favour cards that are good in long games ([Star City Games](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/select/using-17lands-com-as-a-resource-to-improve-at-limited/)), and causal readings of them face confounding and selection effects ([arXiv 2604.18314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18314)). Three probe biases remain, all *inference*. The forecast's scale is wrong late in the game, so late probes overstate magnitudes. The insertion moment is off the natural distribution of when a card is held. And effects beyond the current round reach the score only through the forecast. Improvements in order of cost: apply the per-round calibration map to both arms before taking the difference; use the two existing play-outs per probe to separate between-state variance from play-out variance, and if between-state variance dominates, switch to one play-out and twice as many probes; shrink low-count cards toward the pool mean before ranking them, since a 16-probe card has about 2.5× the standard error of a 98-probe card; and occasionally play a subset of probes to the end of the game to estimate the remaining bias directly. Fisher's usage test (a card always bought by optimised bots is too strong, one never bought is too weak) is a free cross-check from draft logs ([Fisher](https://graphics.stanford.edu/~mdfisher/DominionAI.html)).

## Tune several weights at once against a pool, not one at a time against the table

Hand-tuning one weight per challenger run is a (1+1) evolution strategy with too little re-evaluation, and it has the same noise problem as everything above (*inference*). The optimiser with direct game-AI evidence at our budget is **NTBEA** (N-Tuple Bandit Evolutionary Algorithm). It keeps bandit statistics for every single parameter value, every pair, and the full tuple; proposes 50 neighbours; and picks the one with the highest UCB ([Goodman & Lucas, arXiv 2003.10378](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.10378)). In Planet Wars, with **5 parameters and 288 single-game evaluations**, 60% of runs recommended a top-6 setting out of 228. With 8 parameters and 1,000 evaluations it reached a mean true win rate of 0.707. **Several short runs plus a high-sample re-evaluation of each recommendation beat one long run** ([arXiv 2003.10378](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.10378)). It significantly outperformed grid search in its original paper ([QMUL](https://gameai.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/2018/06/15/lucas2018ntbea/)), was "competitive" with SMAC ([arXiv 1901.00723](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.00723)), and was used to tune MCTS across the 8 TAG tabletop games ([arXiv 2202.05773](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2202.05773)). Its by-product, a table of which parameter values and pairs matter, is useful in its own right to a designer.

The rejected alternatives are rejected for budget reasons. **SPSA** needs tens of thousands of games and "does not converge": unimportant variables random-walk ([CPW SPSA](https://www.chessprogramming.org/SPSA); [CPW Stockfish tuning](https://chessprogramming.org/Stockfish%27s_Tuning_Method)). **CLOP** has similar verification costs that grow with the number of parameters, and its only performance evidence is its author's own ([CPW CLOP](https://chessprogramming.org/CLOP)). No game-AI budget figures exist for CMA-ES at our scale ([arXiv 2409.16757](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16757v3)). NTBEA's evidence stops at 8 parameters, so none of ours covers all 15 weights at 300 games. Kiiski's advice settles the question: **choose which variables to tune**, and tie related values to shared knobs ([CPW](https://chessprogramming.org/Stockfish%27s_Tuning_Method)). A plan that fits about 8 hours on 8 cores (*inference*): about 30 seeded baseline games to estimate variance; two NTBEA runs of about 80 games each over 5 to 8 weights at 3 to 4 levels, with the rest frozen; then about 100 games re-evaluating the top 2 or 3 recommendations against the standard, in duplicate blocks. The implementation is roughly 150 lines of JS around a Map keyed by tuple strings. With five seats, one game can seat several candidates at once, which cuts the cost further.

The backers-versus-trophy see-saw is the textbook symptom of tuning each profile against one fixed table. AlphaStar's league trains against a pool of past and current agents with prioritised fictitious self-play, which weights opponents by how often they beat the agent. The pool alone was not enough, so dedicated exploiters were added to find weaknesses ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning/)). Nash averaging rates agents against the equilibrium mixture of the payoff matrix and is not skewed by redundant weak entries ([Balduzzi et al., arXiv 1806.02643](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.02643)). Diplomacy research found multiple equilibria, with self-play alone possibly insufficient ([DORA](https://api.deepai.org/publication/no-press-diplomacy-from-scratch)). That is a warning that bot-only tuning can settle into conventions humans will not follow. Hearthstone balancing rests on the same premise: a balance verdict means something only when each archetype is played near its best response ([arXiv 1907.06562](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.06562)). For us (*inference*): keep a pool of frozen standard versions of both profiles, fill the other four seats from that pool, accept a change only if it gains against the pool, and after each profile is tuned, run a short best-response tune of the other profile. If that best response gains a lot, the "even at 20%" reading was not an equilibrium. A batch Plackett–Luce fit over finishing orders, with confidence intervals from bootstrapping whole seeds, rates all variants from the same games and suits a fixed set of bots better than TrueSkill ([OpenSkill](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.05901.pdf); [Yeung et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.16565)). A rank or margin reward carries more information per game than win/lose. Because the bots play to win, rank below first only partly reflects strength, so use it for screening and wins for adoption (*inference*).

## The current search is close to what the evidence favours; spend its budget better

Our bot is a form of perfect-information Monte Carlo (PIMC) with flat Monte Carlo at the root and heuristic play-outs to round end, followed by an evaluation. The literature mostly endorses that shape. PIMC is "state of the art in various trick-taking card games" despite strategy fusion ([EPIMC, arXiv 2408.02380](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2408.02380)). Long et al. show that PIMC succeeds when hidden information is revealed quickly and sibling outcomes are correlated ([AAAI 2010](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/7562)), and EPIMC found that **deeper hidden-information reasoning gave no clear gain in games with mostly public observations** ([arXiv 2408.02380](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2408.02380)). ISMCTS fixes strategy fusion, but on Dou Di Zhu it showed no significant difference from determinized MCTS ([MCTS review, arXiv 2103.04931](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.04931)). For five players, MCTS with max^n backups (each seat maximising its own entry of a reward vector) is the evidence-backed structure ([Nijssen thesis](https://project.dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/games/files/phd/Nijssen_thesis.pdf); [Sturtevant 2008](https://www.cs.du.edu/~sturtevant/papers/sturtevant2008analysis.html)). That is what "each seat plays its own profile" approximates. MCTS is also robust to inaccurate opponent models ([Goodman & Lucas, arXiv 2006.08659](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08659v1)), and SPARTA proved that searching for one agent while the others follow a known blueprint does at least as well as the blueprint, raising Hanabi scores for every agent tested ([Lerer et al., arXiv 1912.02318](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02318v1)).

The direct warning concerns play-out quality. Nijssen and Winands found that running small searches inside play-outs improves play-out quality but **slows them enough to cancel the gain at short thinking times** ([Playout search](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/playout-search-for-monte-carlo-tree-search-in-multi-player-games)). At about 13 minutes a game, the fast play-out bots should stay fast. The better levers are budget allocation and cost per play-out (*inference*; I retrieved no primary source on sequential halving). `simPick` already shares play-out seeds across candidates, which is the right common-random-numbers practice. It then spends `keep × playouts` evenly. **Successive halving** gives all k candidates one or two play-outs, drops the worse half, and doubles the play-outs for the rest. It targets "which move is best" rather than estimating every candidate equally, and is a change of about 20 lines inside `simPick`. Either it buys more play-outs on close calls at the same cost, or it gives the same decisions in about half the time. That trade should be gated with a non-regression SPRT. `cardWorth` calls `structuredClone`, and cloning usually dominates play-out cost. Profiling for copy-on-write state is the other speed lever (*inference*). Inside a single move, root parallelisation does not raise tuning throughput, because the cores already run separate games (*inference*).

On hidden information, the honest upgrade is cheap. **Replace peeking with determinization**: before each play-out, redeal the unseen cards (opponents' hands, face-down cards, the deck) consistently with what the bot knows, including the cards it passed to each neighbour in the draft (*inference*). That is the kind of hard deduction DeepRole uses ([arXiv 1906.02330](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02330)). Skat programs go further and weight sampled worlds by consistency with opponents' actions ([Sturtevant Skat](https://cs.du.edu/~sturtevant/papers/skat.pdf); [arXiv 1903.09604](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.09604.pdf)). Its main payoff is that bot results will hold up when humans sit at the table and that card probes stop crediting knowledge no player has. It will probably make the bots slightly weaker in bot-only play, so measure it with a non-regression test, not a gainer test. A per-opponent belief over {trophy, backer of faction X}, updated from move frequencies per profile already in the logs, is the Prob-maxn idea ([Sturtevant, Prob-Maxn](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~nathanst/papers/probmaxn.pdf)). It matters only once peeking at seats' profiles is removed. Strategy fusion bites hardest on face-down bluff decisions, where a peeking searcher always "knows" the truth (*inference*). DeepNash's success shows unpredictability is worth something ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/mastering-stratego-the-classic-game-of-imperfect-information/)), so a small randomised bluff rate is worth testing as a variant once bots infer.

Two evaluator-side ideas round this out. **Texel-style tuning** fits evaluation weights to final outcomes over logged positions without playing new games. In chess it gained about +100 Elo cumulatively, and its largest single gain (+39) came from choosing which positions to include ([CPW Texel](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Texel%27s_Tuning_Method)). It sets evaluation weights, not behavioural persona weights such as jitter, and needs splitting by game and validation by play (*inference*). On the draft, Draftsim's expert bot (strength plus a commitment bonus that is small while uncommitted and large once committed) matched human picks at **44.5%, against 48.7% for a neural net** ([Ward et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2009.00655)). Pick accuracy was lowest mid-pack, where synergy dominates. Fisher found that buy decisions matter far more than play decisions in Dominion ([Fisher](https://graphics.stanford.edu/~mdfisher/DominionAI.html)). That suggests measuring how much of our win variance comes from the draft before adding play-time search (*inference*). For the trophy profile, a commitment bonus would point at the colour that most raises the weakest colour, the reverse of Magic's concentration (*inference*). Any new draft term should start as a variant seeded by the designer, per the project's working rules.

Not recommended at our speed: SPSA and CLOP (budgets of tens of thousands of games), TD learning or neural evaluators (TD-Gammon used about 1.5M games ([Tesauro](https://www.ece.uvic.ca/~bctill/papers/learning/Tesauro_1995.pdf))), giving play-out bots their own search, full multi-observer ISMCTS, and paranoid or Best-Reply search, whose advantages come from alpha-beta pruning and do not carry over to MCTS ([Nijssen thesis](https://project.dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/games/files/phd/Nijssen_thesis.pdf)).

The main uncertainties are three. No source measures how much variance duplicate play or AIVAT removes in a 5-player board game. No source tests NTBEA beyond 8 parameters. No source measures belief tracking in a game shaped like ours. Each of these needs a short measurement run here before a large commitment.

## Conclusion

The central change is to stop treating game count as the unit of evidence and treat information per game as the thing to raise. Paired seeds, a luck correction built from the bots' own forecast, and a stopping rule could plausibly turn today's 37 games an hour into the evidential equivalent of several times that. That makes the designer's quick-iteration style safe rather than misleading: small runs screen, sequential tests decide. The bots' biggest weakness, the over-confident late forecast, is also the cheapest to fix, because the outcome data to calibrate it already sits in the logs. Fixing it improves play, probes and any variance correction that uses the forecast.

Two results should shape what comes next. Balance between backers and trophy cannot be read off a single table, because each profile tuned against the other's current version is exploiting a snapshot. A pool and a best-response check are the minimum for any balance claim fed back into the design document. And the evidence says MotW's search already has about the right shape for a quickly revealed, mostly public game. Further strength will come from spending play-outs where decisions are close and from measuring whether the draft or the play decides games, not from deeper or more elaborate search.
